# Radio conversations and radio imaginaries

*2026.08.31 – 2026.09.05*

## MIRAGES

Pillars of smoke rise soundlessly on the horizon. They seem to melt into it, their shapes smeared and distorted against the line separating the sea from the sky. Twisted and gigantic, they stand immobile in the afternoon heat, and their image is mirrored on the transparent, lucid surface beneath them. The air is dense, still and oily, magnifying our vision; it flickers and suspends them between these two rarefied worlds.

## DREAM OF EARHART

We set out on a search for the radio that belonged to Earhart. She was the first woman to fly solo across the Atlantic, and in 1937, when she tried to cross the Pacific, her plane went down. It was never found. Ghost radio programmes have been transmitting from her radio ever since.

The other members of the crew seem to have the exact coordinates, though; they were given them in their dreams. When we reach the spot, she plunges. We watch her body magnify and liquefy as she goes under, then grow smaller and darker as she descends into the abyss. We are far out on the high seas, the deep seas. The sky is grey and so is the water. A slight wind is blowing, but the boat is almost still. We lower the sails and let ourselves float.

She disappears for what seems like an eternity, then emerges holding something in her hands, his hands, radiant. We crowd around the device. It is quite big, rectangular with very rounded edges, and quite flat. It has two antennas at its ends, long, shiny and still intact. She holds it with both hands. We are all wearing dark wetsuit caps, and our round heads are the only things visible on the surface of the ocean: six black dots crowding around this radio from the past.

The extraordinary thing is that it still emits sounds. It is still on. Neither water nor time has exhausted its inner workings or its battery. We feel a sense of magic. The sounds seem more passive than active, crackling and interrupted. She wants to stay a little longer with the radio and let herself be carried away by its soothing sounds. The others go back on board.

We lie down on the ocean. Her head on my chest, my hand in her hair. We look at the grey sky and listen to Earhart's radio; she holds it tight against her heart. The sea is so soft and comfortable, gently receiving our backs. If we could, we would stay here for eternity.

## VOICES IN THE DISTANCE

3 a.m. I finish my night watch, making sure our course is clear, tell ◍ there is some warm tea in the thermos, and go into the cabin. I lie down comfortably in the corner between the bunk and the hull, as we are heeling to port. After a while, I am woken by the crackling of the VHF radio, set to channel 16. A cargo ship's officer is shouting at a woman on a sailing vessel, telling her to urgently change her heading to starboard, or they will collide. She agrees, sounding stressed. We can't see anything around us; they are too far away to be seen in the darkness, but their radio presence is felt.

**⭙ We hear people we don't know talking to each other on channel 16 in the middle of the night. There is no privacy, which is quite unusual in the modern world we live in…**

⬭ I mean, it's very usual in this modern world: there's no privacy on social media either.

◓ I think it's creepy, but in a nice way. It's like getting a glimpse of other people's lives.

◐ I think it's a good idea for safety reasons. But I find it very annoying when two boats decide to have a conversation on channel 16 without moving to another channel, and you're forced to listen to them until they're finished.

◍ Sometimes someone calls your boat's name over the channel, and you get stressed thinking there's a problem, but it turns out they're just bored in the middle of the Atlantic. Then, once the stress has passed, it's quite nice to chat with someone you don't know. It's like having a pen pal.

## VHF AND ANTENNAS

**⭙ Which radio devices do we have on board?**

⬭ VHF radio, HF radio (not that common), antenna, AIS, radar, Garmin satellite phone…

**⭙ What is VHF radio?**

◒ A radio that's used to communicate with anything within a relatively close area.

◍ It's a radio with different channels, for either broadcast or two-way communication.

◓ It works on the principle of electromagnetism. The electrons wiggle about and communicate with each other. When they're less dense, that's a lower frequency, so less information is carried. VHF is very high frequency, so it can carry more information, but it doesn't transmit as far.

**⭙ What is an antenna? How does it work?**

⬬ It's like a big ear.

◓ It looks like a weird metal pole, or sometimes a dish, at the top of a mast or a house.

⬭ It's a big stick pointing into the sky that receives and transmits. It's tall and skinny, and usually at the back of the boat.

◍ I think it's usually placed high up, at the top of the mast, for receiving and transmitting radio waves. It can transmit and receive over long ranges because the waves are long enough to bend around the Earth.

## OTHERWISE USES

**⭙ Imagine if we used VHF radio for other activities, beyond pragmatic purposes…**

◓ That would be funny. We could read poems on some channels, so all the cargo ship crews would be soothed.

⬬ Technically, you're not allowed to transmit messages over VHF radio unless you have a licence. When I used to work in a refugee camp, the only way we had to communicate was VHF radio, and there I could speak because I had a call sign: ROMEO1. I really like my call sign.

## POSITION TRACKING

7 p.m. The navigation screen shows our position off the coast. No other vessel is visible on the horizon; the sun is starting to set. I realise the AIS is off. I go down into the cabin and switch it on. Beep… beeep… beeeep. After several seconds, one after another, little spiky shapes start populating my map: black outlines piled on top of each other in the port far behind us, and scattered all over the ocean. The radio waves of the AIS let us see what our eyes cannot.

**⭙ How does AIS work?**

⬭ It stands for Automatic Identification Service, maybe? I think you send signals to a satellite and then they bounce back?

**⭙ And what about satellite phones?**

⬭ Satellite phones allow very long-distance, and very expensive, phone calls.

◍ AIS is used for tracking boats. Satellite phones are used to communicate without Wi-Fi or mobile data. I once used satellite email (I don't know if that's the same as a satellite phone) to send my family updates midway through an ocean crossing. AIS is only mandatory for cargo and commercial vessels; not all sailing or fishing boats have an AIS call sign.

◐ AIS geolocates you with GPS and displays your position on a map. In an emergency, it sends a distress call so you can be rescued.

**⭙ And who receives the distress call?**

⬬ Well, "they" get it, it's an international convention… Actually, I have no idea…

⬭ The signal goes up into the sky and everyone can see it.

◑ It travels so far that the whole universe knows… all the way to Mars. There's some sort of creature up there in the stratosphere that receives all the calls. It has lots of limbs, which it uses to read all the red threads and send the messages back down to Earth. It knows exactly which coast guard to send them to.

## DEPENDENCE

**⭙ We will be relying heavily on radio devices as we cross the Atlantic. If the AIS stops working, the Starlink subscription gets cut off, or the VHF antenna breaks, our dependence on them will become evident. How do you feel about this?**

⬬ Good! I would love to have the skills and knowledge to open it up and rebuild it if it failed. But I also like the idea of sailing without any means of communication, completely disconnected. If our destiny is to get lost in a storm, fall overboard or get hit by an orca… we should just surrender to it and accept it.

⬭ It's a very good point. Even for the licence you need to speak on marine radio, you learn how to operate it and how to deal with the technology, but you don't really understand the mechanisms and physical principles behind it. This idea of autonomy in the face of failure is something we should think about more. The more we think about it, the more absurd it becomes that we depend so heavily on devices like phones and tablets without knowing how they work. We hold these black rectangles in our hands at all times and perform actions with them. If aliens came to Earth and looked at us, I think we would look so funny, all concentrating on these black rectangles.

◍ I believe these technologies exist for a reason, so we might as well use them. If we think we should be knowledgeable in every field, we'll go crazy; it's impossible. As a captain, I believe all these devices on board make life easier and help us navigate, but they aren't essential to navigation itself. We should always, first and foremost, be able to read the wind, the clouds and the waves, and make decisions based on concrete observation rather than relying on electronic devices. We have to be aware that they can fail, and keep an accurate and frequent log, including our GPS position, so we can navigate with paper charts if necessary.

◓ Surprisingly okay (my family probably won't be so happy). I feel weirdly relaxed about being even more cut off from the outside world and just having to be more vigilant and careful on watch. My only worry is if we ran into a problem and had no way of calling for help.

## RADAR

We look at the heatmap the radar produces on the navigation screen. Red stains smear across the concentric rings, like burst blood vessels, or the magma bubbles of a slowly erupting volcano. Zooming in, zooming out.

◓ Do you see the stains on the left of the screen? That's the land in the distance. Everything else could be boats, if it matches an AIS point, or bad weather coming in.

⬭ It looks like an ultrasound.

⬬ Well, it's a bit the same principle. The radar spins around (that's why it uses more energy than the other electronic devices on board) and sends out sound waves that bounce off objects and come back, making this concentric map.

## LONG RANGES

◑ In the case of VHF, waves don't bend around the Earth. Only HF and longer waves do that, and they bounce off the ionosphere. They don't use satellites, only the Earth's natural field.

◍ They're lower-frequency waves. The radio on board that works on this principle is the SSB radio.

◒ Yes, and at night, as the ionosphere gets thinner, radio waves can travel almost twice as far.

**⭙ Ah, cool… but why? What natural elements influence it?**

⬭ Maybe the wind? Maybe it helps carry the waves?

◓ I'd guess it's humid air, since more water in the air might block or reflect the waves…

◍ In the past we used to receive weather faxes and emails via SSB, but satellite phones have pretty much replaced it. When we were in Svalbard, the only way to talk to the outside world was the satellite phone. We had only one subscription for the whole boat, so everybody could see everyone's messages. One crew member didn't realise this and sent love messages to his partner every night. When he found out everybody could read them, he didn't seem too bothered, to be honest. A sailing boat is a place with little privacy anyway.

⬬ We also used SSB in Antarctica, before the Starlink era, to communicate with the other research stations, which were several days away, too far for VHF range. In summer we also used to talk to incoming pilots. I've taken quite a few ham radio courses, and lots of people were trying to make contact with the International Space Station (400 km above the ground) on its amateur radio channel. You need to calculate the right time to call, as it passes over where you are on Earth only every so often. Sometimes they reply and say hi.

◒ Lightning also produces these kinds of low-frequency waves. We could listen to them with the right instruments…

◑ Lightning is dangerous, though, as it always hits the highest point, which is us, in the middle of the ocean. Some sailors install a box on top of the mast that's supposed to emit an electromagnetic field to push the lightning away. I don't know if it really works, but we could try.
