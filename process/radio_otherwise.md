# Interview with Radio Otherwise

*Monai de Paula Antunes, Kate Donovan and Niko de Paula Lefort.*

**How did you become so interested in radio? What inspires you about it?**

**Kate:** For me it started with relations: relations with the environment, but also with people. We began this as a seminar in 2021, and slowly it became Radio Otherwise. I was working within a radio community and doing sound art, and I started thinking about waves, about materiality, about connection. Different species perceive the electromagnetic spectrum in completely different ways, and that's something I kept coming back to.

My master's thesis was on the Anthropocene, on human-generated material. We talk endlessly about concrete and plastics, but we almost never think of electromagnetism as part of that same story. I was also drawn to feminist practices, to the non-Western origins of radio, and more specifically to amateur and pirate radio.

Then there's the Titanic, the disaster that effectively started the regulation of the radio spectrum. Women were often blamed for these kinds of disasters, and the way women's and men's voices were treated on air is interesting in itself. Women were considered unable to handle a disaster, and were largely pushed out of the field.

**Monai and Niko:** We're all over the place, really, because radio gives you that possibility. As an artist group we've been playing around with it for a while, but at some point it's nice to become more concrete: to choose where you want to listen, which frequency, which part of the spectrum. You hear very different things depending on where you are. On the islands around Helsinki it's completely different from an urban environment. It opens up another cartography entirely.

**Radio seems to be fading in the digital era. Why do people still use it, and how is amateur radio regulated?**

There's a whole radio community, and within it a female voice might get no attention, or more attention, depending on the space. A huge part of amateur radio is simply about listening. People announce their call sign, and if you pick it up, you send them a card confirming the reception. It's all about the strength of the signal, its clarity, and the distance it has travelled.

Amateur radio has its own regulations, but they're largely upheld through self-policing. If someone isn't playing by the rules, they get reported by the community itself.

**Do you visualise radio waves? If so, how?**

**Kate:** I don't know if I visualise them at all. When I try, I feel it's already a simplification.

**Monai:** When I try, I think of something very complex, a kind of micro-environment. It's almost too complex to properly imagine.

**Together:** We often think of bioacoustic soundscapes. Every living body carries its own signal. A garden is a good parallel for us, because it's a complex ecosystem where everything depends on everything else. We like to bring radio into that same kind of relation, even on the ocean.

**Do you know of any works that bring together radio waves and the sea?**

Pirate radio in the 1960s was all about boats, going offshore, into a kind of no man's land. Even the name "pirate radio" comes from that.

**What's the easiest way to build a listening device I could bring on a boat? What about antennas?**

For a VLF antenna, we can send you the recipe, but you'll probably only end up listening to the ionosphere.

The crystal radio is the simplest receiver there is: just one diode, plus a ground connection. You can add a coil, which works as the tuning coil. There's an old book on this, too. You should also look at Ioana Vreme Moser, an artist working with radio, connected to the Locus Sonus project in Aix-en-Provence and Marseille.

**Kate:** My advice would be to buy a CB (a Citizens Band radio, the hardcore walkie-talkie type). Legally, you're allowed to just talk into it.
