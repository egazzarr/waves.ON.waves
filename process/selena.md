# Interview with Selena Savić

*Author of* Radio Explorations.

**Your book *Radio Explorations* is a fascinating blend of disciplines, spanning from architecture to machine learning. How did you become interested in radio waves in the first place?**

Artists, engineers and architects have been looking at radio waves from a variety of perspectives. While I was working at ATTP (Architecture Theory and Philosophy of Space) in Vienna, I became interested in the materiality of technology and data. That's where this attempt to gather an interdisciplinary outlook on the topic came from.

I worked with a programmer and developer, Carl Coltena, to investigate how men — mostly men — around the world record and collect radio signals. He would look at the signal waterfall on a screen and, just from its shape, could tell me where it came from, what kind of environment it was in, what kind of signal it was, and so on. The signals were very embodied. It's crazy: we live in a world permeated by electromagnetic signals, yet it's as if we were blind to them.

**In the world of AI, or at least in the science I'm most familiar with, a "black box" is an algorithm, more specifically a neural network. In your book's introduction you talk about another kind of black box, which goes back to the post-WWII definition of Wiener and the cybernetic theorists, later described by Latour as "the old kind of black box." What is a black box to you? And what do you mean by "what happens if we open it and find nothing there"?**

The black box is a useful concept — a thinking tool, a trick. Back in the '50s and '60s, in the era of cybernetics, it was a liberating tool: it let you enclose certain processes by looking only at the input and the output. It freed whoever was using an apparatus from the responsibility of knowing what was inside the box, and let them believe it was reliable. It was also about delegation, since the cybernetic thinkers were much more interested in theoretical computational frameworks than in the engineering behind them.

The black box can also be interpreted in very different ways. In some pedagogical circles, for example, it's perfectly acceptable to talk about a child's brain as a black box, a useful concept for separating what's inside from what's outside. I somewhat disagree with this notion. I think that when the teacher opens the black box of my kid, she'll find nothing there — apart from a brain.

The black box is a secret, a mystery. It's both a tool of power and a thinking tool, a form of resistance to the idea that you can open everything up. Opening the black box is a demand for accountability, but maybe we should be looking at something else instead. Keeping the ambiguity intact is sometimes what allows us to keep on living. You can never arrive at purity; chasing it would be exhausting.

**Your interview with Douglas Kahn, author of *Earth Sound Earth Signal*, is a poetic description of radio signals emanating from natural phenomena: lightning, the ionosphere, the Earth itself, at the lowest frequencies, in the hertz range. It opens up a line of research into elemental media studies that I'm very interested in exploring. What makes his work important to you?**

Douglas Kahn is one of the first, and rare, people to theorise long-range radio signals in the field of art. It's extremely important to underline that he didn't approach them scientifically, but artistically: producing sounds and interacting creatively with natural electromagnetic signals.

I think this is worth noting because in science, new works and new theories don't need to be justified; their place in our rational culture is ensured by the way the world currently works. In art, however, works have to fight for a place and a space, and everything has to be rigorously justified.

**Pirate radio historically began as an act of resistance against cultural regulation. Radio has always been used during protests; it's often the only way to communicate in remote areas; radio spoofing is common in war zones; and low-frequency mesh networks offer a decentralised way to spread information outside centralised control. The ocean, as an unoccupied territory, could be seen as a natural site for resistance and piracy. You don't investigate this spatial relationship directly in the book, but you do discuss resistance through feminist theory. Can you expand on that?**

Yes. In my article (Savić 2022) I talk about resisting the dominant understanding of data as something discrete, and treating it instead as relational and material. It's also a resistance to the idea that everything must have a purpose — to the instrumentalisation of physics itself. Hertz, when he first produced and detected radio waves, famously said he didn't think they would ever have any practical application.

Something interesting I've noticed about decentralised networks, from taking part in workshops where you build your own DIY mesh network, is how the technology keeps people occupied simply with the act of seeing and reaching each other.

WWII is full of this kind of radio resistance: clandestine listening, clandestine broadcasting, information moving against the control of the occupying power. At the end of the war, when the partisans were liberating cities from the fascists — on Vis, in Croatia, for example — the first thing they did was take the hills where the radio antennas stood, to take over communication. People also reportedly kept illegal radio receivers specifically to listen to broadcasts from the other side, as an act of informational resistance under occupation. Later, pirate radio took on a different but related role: giving a voice to under-represented communities that mainstream broadcasting excluded.

Another good historical example is the Soviet Union's **Duga** system, built in the 1970s. It was a massive over-the-horizon radar array, nicknamed the "Russian Woodpecker" by shortwave listeners worldwide because of the rhythmic tapping its signal produced across the HF bands. It wasn't a known or publicly acknowledged technology at the time. Its existence was pieced together largely by amateur radio operators who kept hearing this unexplained interference and started speculating — some thought it was Soviet mind control, others weather manipulation — until hobbyists correctly identified it as a radar system, well before any government officially confirmed it. It's a nice case of a secret piece of Cold War infrastructure being partially "opened" not by spies or journalists, but by amateurs who were simply annoyed by interference on their radios.

**Amateur radio communities have definitely driven real technical advances. I'm looking into amateur communities who record long-range radio signals and try to push their range as far as possible, but I mostly find US-centric references.**

There's a special kind of hell reserved for this kind of observation. It's almost always white men with their high-tech instruments and their free time. They venture out into the world, record these signals in an amateur, passive way, and then put them online.

I'm not trying to condemn them for it. I think everyone should be aware of the signals that permeate the space we inhabit, and everyone should go out and build their own antenna, if only to understand how radio communication actually works and to become intellectually independent of it as a black box. But the fact remains that radio amateurs today are, very often, white American men.
